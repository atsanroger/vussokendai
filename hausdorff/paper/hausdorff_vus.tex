\title{Certified bounds on the inclusive $\tau\to X_s\nu_\tau$ rate\\ from lattice correlators with a Hausdorff moment condition on $[-1,1]$}
\author{Wei-Lun Chen\\[0.2em]
{\small Working draft. Derivations and numerics were prepared with Claude (Anthropic)}\\{\small and are to be checked line by line.}}
\date{26 October 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Extracting $\Vus$ from the inclusive decay $\tau\to X_s\nu_\tau$ requires an integral of the $\bar us$ spectral function against a known kinematic kernel.
The lattice provides only the Euclidean correlator, and inverting it is ill-posed. The Chebyshev method addresses this by assuming that the correlator is a positive
combination of $\lambda^t$ with $\lambda=\ee^{-E}\in(0,1]$. For domain-wall fermions with $M_5>1$ the transfer matrix has negative eigenvalues, so this assumption
can fail. We find that it does fail on RBC/UKQCD data: the time-component ($J=0$) correlators cannot be reproduced by a positive measure on $(0,1]$ within $3\sigma$.
They can be reproduced by a positive measure on $[-1,1]$.
We then replace the truncated Chebyshev expansion by a different question: over all positive measures on $[-1,1]$ that reproduce the data, how large and how small
can the kernel integral be? This is a linear program. Its dual is a polynomial that lies above (below) the kernel, and that polynomial certifies the bound independently of the solver.
On the $48^3\times96$ and $64^3\times128$ ensembles, with the HFLAV value $\BR_s=0.02908(48)$, we obtain $0.198\le\Vus\le0.249$ ($0.196$--$0.251$ including the $\BR_s$ error).
This interval is a guarantee under stated assumptions, not a precision determination.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The inclusive width $\Gamma(\tau\to X_s\nu_\tau)$ is proportional to $\Vus^2$ times an integral of the $\bar us$ spectral function $\rho(Q^2)$ against a kernel fixed by kinematics.
If $\rho$ were known, $\Vus$ would follow from the measured branching fraction. On the lattice we have the Euclidean correlator $C(t)$, which is the Laplace transform of $\rho$, and
recovering an integral of $\rho$ from noisy values of $C(t)$ is an ill-posed inverse problem.

The approach of Refs.~\cite{Hashimoto:2017,Bailas:2020,Gambino:2020} avoids an explicit inversion. One writes $C(t)$ as the expectation of $\lambda^t$, $\lambda=\ee^{-\hat H}$, expands the kernel in
shifted Chebyshev polynomials $T^*_n(\lambda)$, and determines the expectations $\langle T^*_n\rangle$ from the data. The key input is that $\lambda^t$ is averaged over a \emph{positive measure
on $[0,1]$}. That input implies $|\langle T^*_n\rangle|\le1$, which keeps the expansion under control.

Applying this method to the RBC/UKQCD $\bar us$ correlators, we ran into three problems:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item The ensembles use M\"obius domain-wall fermions with $M_5=1.8$. For $M_5>1$ the one-step transfer matrix has real \emph{negative} eigenvalues \cite{Syritsyn:2007}.
      A positive measure on $[0,1]$ is then not guaranteed.
\item In the original implementation the fitted $\langle T^*_n\rangle$ leave $[-1,1]$ already at $n\simeq5$--$8$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:cheb}). The resulting $\Vus$ changes by tens of percent from one truncation order to the next.
\item The kernel diverges like $1/E$ at $E\to0$, and the original code regulates it by hand. Physically no state lies below the kaon, so a lower edge $E\ge M_K$ is available and makes the regulator unnecessary.
\end{enumerate}

This note proposes a remedy that keeps the logic of the calculation but changes two things. First, the support of the measure is enlarged from $(0,1]$ to $[-1,1]$, which is what the transfer matrix allows.
We test this hypothesis directly on the data with Hausdorff moment conditions. Second, the truncated expansion is replaced by an optimisation. Instead of one estimate with an uncontrolled truncation error,
we compute the \emph{largest and smallest} kernel integral compatible with the data and the hypothesis. The answer is an interval, and each end comes with a polynomial certificate that can be checked independently.

\paragraph{Outline.} Sec.~\ref{sec:summary} summarises the method and the result on one page. Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} writes the decay rate as a linear functional of the correlator and fixes all normalisations.
Sec.~\ref{sec:support} discusses the support of the spectral measure. Sec.~\ref{sec:bounds} is the core of the note and develops the bounds in detail. Sec.~\ref{sec:hausdorff} tests the hypothesis,
Sec.~\ref{sec:cheb} explains why the old Chebyshev coefficients leave $[-1,1]$, Sec.~\ref{sec:results} gives the results, and Sec.~\ref{sec:limits} states what is and what is not shown.

\subsection{The method in one page}\label{sec:summary}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Data.} The eight $\gamma$ structures of the $\bar us$ correlator are grouped into $A_T,V_T$ (temporal, $J=0$) and $A_S,V_S$ (spatial, $J=1$).
      After renormalisation and division by the volume, $C_{\rm ren}=Z^2C_{\rm file}/L^3$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:norm}), the rate is a linear functional of the correlator:
      $\hat\Sigma_{\rm tot}=\sum_c\int\dd\hat E\,(2/\hat E)K_J\hat\rho_c$ and $\Gapp=(a^{-1})^2\hat\Sigma$ (Eqs.~\ref{eq:Gapp_E}--\ref{eq:channels}).
\item \textbf{Hypothesis.} $C(t)=\int\dd\sigma(\lambda)\,\lambda^{t-1}$ for $t\ge1$, with $\sigma\ge0$ supported on $[-1,\ee^{-M_0}]$ and $M_0$ the kaon mass (Eq.~\ref{eq:model}).
      The old method assumes support on $(0,\ee^{-M_0}]$.
\item \textbf{Constraints.} The moments $m_k=\int\lambda^k\dd\sigma$ must lie in $C(k+1)\pm z\delta_{k+1}$ for $k=0,\dots,T-1$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:primal}).
\item \textbf{Bounds.} The largest and smallest $\int g\,\dd\sigma$ over all such $\sigma$ are solutions of a linear program. Its dual is a polynomial $p\ge g$ (or $q\le g$) that
      certifies the bound (Sec.~\ref{sec:bounds}). Negative-$\lambda$ states can absorb part of the moments, but they never contribute to the rate.
\item \textbf{Rate.} $\Vus=\sqrt{\BR_s\Gamma_\tau\big/\big(S_{EW}\tfrac{G_F^2m_\tau^3}{16\pi}\Sigma_{\rm tot}\big)}$, with
      $\BR_s=0.02908(48)$ from HFLAV 2023 (Sec.~11.6.1 of arXiv:2411.18639).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Result.} On the RBC/UKQCD $48^3\times96$ and $64^3\times128$ ensembles, a positive measure on $(0,1]$ cannot reproduce the $J=0$ channels within $3\sigma$
(it needs $z^*=3.3$--$19$), while one on $[-1,1]$ can ($z^*\le0.1$). The certified interval is $0.198\le\Vus\le0.249$ (both ensembles, $z=3$, $T=18$), or
$0.196\le\Vus\le0.251$ with the $\BR_s$ error included. It contains the HFLAV lattice-inclusive value $0.2189(19)$ and the OPE value $0.2184(21)$.
The interval is about $\pm11\%$ wide. It is a guarantee, not a precision determination.

\paragraph{Conventions.} $E$ is an energy and $\lambda=\ee^{-E}$. Lattice quantities are dimensionless and carry a hat where confusion is possible;
$a^{-1}$ converts them to GeV. $T$ is the number of correlator values used, $t=1,\dots,T$.

\section{From the decay rate to a linear functional of the correlator}\label{sec:setup}
This section rewrites the decay rate step by step as $\int g\,\dd\sigma$ for a known function $g$. Each step is checked against the single-state case, where the answer is known in closed form.

\subsection{The rate}
Following Eq.~(28) of Ref.~\cite{Chen:TauNote}, with $y=Q^2/m_\tau^2$ and $\Gamma_0=m_\tau^5G_F^2/(192\pi^3)$,
\begin{equation}
 R\equiv\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma_0}=12\pi^2|V_{us}|^2S_{EW}\int_0^{m_\tau^2}\frac{\dd Q^2}{m_\tau^2}(1-y)^2\Big[(1+2y)\rho^{(1)}(Q^2)+\rho^{(0)}(Q^2)\Big].
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $\Gamma_0$ and using $12\pi^2/(192\pi^3)=1/(16\pi)$ gives
\begin{align}
 \Gamma(\tau\to X_s\nu)&=\Vus^2\,S_{EW}\,\frac{G_F^2m_\tau^3}{16\pi}\;\Gapp,\\
 \Gapp&\equiv\int_0^{m_\tau^2}\dd Q^2\Big[K_1(y)\rho^{(1)}(Q^2)+K_0(y)\rho^{(0)}(Q^2)\Big],\\
 K_1(y)&=(1-y)^2(1+2y)\,\theta(1-y),\qquad K_0(y)=(1-y)^2\,\theta(1-y).
\end{align}
This is Eq.~(44) of Ref.~\cite{Chen:TauNote}, with the measured width $\Gamma_{\rm Exp}=\BR_s\Gamma_\tau$ on the left-hand side.

\paragraph{Check with the kaon pole.} For $\rho^{(0)}=f_K^2\delta(Q^2-M_K^2)$ we get
$\Gapp=f_K^2K_0(M_K^2/m_\tau^2)=f_K^2(1-M_K^2/m_\tau^2)^2$. This reproduces the textbook width
$\Gamma(\tau\to K\nu)=G_F^2f_K^2m_\tau^3(1-M_K^2/m_\tau^2)^2|V_{us}|^2/(16\pi)$.
For the $K^*$, $\rho^{(1)}=f_{K^*}^2\delta(Q^2-M_{K^*}^2)$ gives $f_{K^*}^2(1-y)^2(1+2y)$, again as expected.

\subsection{Spectral representation of the correlator}
The zero-momentum correlator is the Laplace transform of the spectral function:
\begin{equation}
 C(t)=\int_0^\infty\dd Q\,Q^2\rho(Q^2)\,\ee^{-Qt}\;\equiv\;\int_0^\infty\dd E\,\rho_E(E)\,\ee^{-Et},\qquad \rho_E(E)=E^2\rho(E^2).
 \label{eq:spec}
\end{equation}
We fix its normalisation with a single state of mass $M$ and decay constant $f$. Following Eqs.~(48) and (51) of Ref.~\cite{Chen:TauNote}, the renormalised correlator per unit volume is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm ren}(t)\equiv\frac{Z^2\,C_{\rm file}(t)}{L^3}=\frac{Mf^2}{2}\,\ee^{-Mt},
 \qquad f=Z\sqrt{\frac{2A}{ML^3}}\ \ (C_{\rm file}\simeq A\ee^{-Mt}),
 \label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
so $\rho_E=\tfrac12Mf^2\delta(E-M)$. Since $\delta(E-M)=2M\,\delta(E^2-M^2)$, this is
$\rho(Q^2)=\rho_E/E^2=f^2\delta(Q^2-M^2)$, the same normalisation as above. The general case follows by linearity. Here $Z=Z_A=Z_V$.

\subsection{The rate in the energy variable}
Substituting $Q^2=E^2$, $\dd Q^2=2E\,\dd E$ and $\rho(E^2)=\rho_E(E)/E^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \int\dd Q^2K(Q^2)\rho(Q^2)=\int\dd E\,2E\,K\,\frac{\rho_E(E)}{E^2}=\int_0^\infty\dd E\;\frac{2}{E}\,K(E)\,\rho_E(E).
 \label{eq:Gapp_E}
\end{equation}
For the single state, $\rho_E=\tfrac12Mf^2\delta(E-M)$ gives $\frac{2}{M}\cdot\frac12Mf^2K=f^2K$, as it must. The factor $2/E$ is the origin of the divergence at $E\to0$.

\subsection{Lattice units}
Let $\hat E=aE$, and write the renormalised correlator in lattice units as $c(t)=Z^2C_{\rm file}/L^3=\int\dd\hat E\,\hat\rho(\hat E)\ee^{-\hat Et}$.
For a single state $\hat M=aM$ and $\hat f=af$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:norm}). Defining $\hat\Sigma\equiv\int\dd\hat E\,(2/\hat E)K\,\hat\rho$,
\begin{equation}
 \hat\Sigma=\hat f^2K=a^2f^2K=a^2\,\Gapp
 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Gapp=(a^{-1})^2\,\hat\Sigma ,
 \label{eq:units}
\end{equation}
and by linearity this holds for any $\hat\rho$. Note that the conversion factor is $(a^{-1})^2$, not $(a^{-1})^3$. The kernel is evaluated at
$y=(a^{-1}\hat E)^2/m_\tau^2$, so its edge sits at $\hat E=m_\tau a=1.027$ (48I) and $0.753$ (64I).

\subsection{Which components carry $J=0$ and $J=1$}
Decompose the vacuum polarisation as
$\Pi^{\mu\nu}(q)=(q^\mu q^\nu-q^2g^{\mu\nu})\Pi^{(1)}+q^\mu q^\nu\Pi^{(0)}$. At zero spatial momentum, $q=(q^0,\vec0)$ and $q^2=q_0^2$, so
\begin{align}
 \Pi^{00}&=(q_0^2-q^2)\Pi^{(1)}+q_0^2\Pi^{(0)}=q_0^2\,\Pi^{(0)},\\
 \Pi^{ii}&=-q^2g^{ii}\,\Pi^{(1)}=q^2\,\Pi^{(1)}\qquad(\text{no sum over } i,\ g^{ii}=-1).
\end{align}
The temporal components ($A_T=\gamma_5\gamma_4$, $V_T=\gamma_4$) therefore carry $J=0$, and the spatial ones ($A_S$, $V_S$, averaged over $i$) carry $J=1$:
\begin{equation}
 \Sig_{\rm tot}=\sum_{X\in\{A,V\}}\Big[\Sig^{(0)}_{X_T}+\Sig^{(1)}_{X_S}\Big],\qquad
 \Sig^{(J)}_{c}=(a^{-1})^2\!\int\!\dd\hat E\,\frac{2}{\hat E}K_J\,\hat\rho_c .
 \label{eq:channels}
\end{equation}
In the RBC files the gamma labels are $g=1,2,4\to V_{x,y,z}$, $8\to V_T$, $14,13,11\to A_{x,y,z}$ and $7\to A_T$.
The original \texttt{vus()} assigns $J=1$ to both axial terms and $J=0$ to both vector terms. This disagrees with Eq.~(\ref{eq:channels}) and with
the mock-data code in the same file.

\section{The support of the spectral measure}\label{sec:support}
\subsection{The standard assumption}
If the one-step transfer matrix $\hat T=\ee^{-\hat H}$ is Hermitian and positive, it has eigenvalues $\lambda_n=\ee^{-E_n}\in(0,1]$. With overlaps
$c_n=\langle n|J|0\rangle$,
\begin{equation}
 C(t)=\sum_n|c_n|^2\lambda_n^{\,t}=\int\dd\nu(\lambda)\,\lambda^t,\qquad \nu\ge0,\ \ \mathrm{supp}\,\nu\subset(0,1].
\end{equation}
The shifted-Chebyshev method rests on this representation.

\subsection{What changes for $M_5>1$}
Syritsyn and Negele \cite{Syritsyn:2007} construct $\hat T$ for Shamir domain-wall fermions. Its eigenvalues are real, and for $M_5>1$ some of them are negative.
They do not discuss the signs of the spectral weights. If only link reflection positivity holds, the natural representation is
\begin{equation}
 C(t)=\sum_n|c_n|^2\lambda_n^{\,t-1}=\int\dd\sigma(\lambda)\,\lambda^{t-1},\qquad t\ge1,\qquad \sigma=\sum_n|c_n|^2\delta_{\lambda_n}\ge0,
 \quad \mathrm{supp}\,\sigma\subset[-1,1].
 \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The moments of $\sigma$ are the correlator values, $m_k\equiv\int\dd\sigma\,\lambda^k=C(k+1)$ for $k=0,1,\dots$. We drop $C(0)$ because it contains contact terms.
For $\lambda>0$ the measure is related to the spectral density by $\dd\sigma=\lambda\hat\rho\,\dd\hat E$, since
$\int\hat\rho\,\ee^{-\hat Et}\dd\hat E=\int\lambda^{t-1}(\lambda\hat\rho\,\dd\hat E)$. A state with $\lambda<0$ has positive weight in $\sigma$.
In the form $C(t)=\int\lambda^t\,\dd\nu$ it would have negative weight, since $\dd\nu=\dd\sigma/\lambda$.

\paragraph{Two hypotheses.} We compare
$H_{[0,1]}$: $\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma\subset(0,1]$ (the old assumption), and $H_{[-1,1]}$: $\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma\subset[-1,1]$ (the new one).
$H_{[-1,1]}$ is weaker and contains $H_{[0,1]}$. It is \emph{not proven} for M\"obius fermions at $M_5=1.8$, so we test it on the data in Sec.~\ref{sec:hausdorff}.

\paragraph{Parity of the start time.} For the shifted sequence $s_k=C(t_s+k)=\int\dd\sigma\,\lambda^{t_s-1}\lambda^k$ to be the moments of a positive measure
on $[-1,1]$, the factor $\lambda^{t_s-1}$ must be non-negative there. This requires $t_s-1$ even, i.e.\ $t_s$ odd. For even $t_s$ the negative-$\lambda$ states enter with negative weight,
and positivity tests are expected to fail. The old method normalises with $2t_0=1$, an odd start time.

\section{Certified bounds: the new method}\label{sec:bounds}
This section is the core of the note. Sec.~\ref{sec:idea} states the idea without formulas. Secs.~\ref{sec:problem}--\ref{sec:dual} set up the optimisation and derive the certificate.
Sec.~\ref{sec:toy} works the method out by hand for two moments. Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics} describes what the code does and which step makes the result rigorous.
Sec.~\ref{sec:extremal} shows what the extremal spectra look like on the data, and Sec.~\ref{sec:recipe} collects the steps.

\subsection{The idea}\label{sec:idea}
The data fix finitely many numbers, $C(1),\dots,C(T)$, each with an error. Many spectral measures reproduce them, and these measures give different values of the kernel integral
$\hat\Sigma$. The Chebyshev method picks \emph{one} of them, through a truncated expansion and a prior, and quotes a central value whose truncation error is not controlled.
We ask a different question: \emph{over all positive measures on the allowed support that reproduce the data within their errors, what are the largest and the smallest possible $\hat\Sigma$?}
The answer is an interval that contains the true value whenever the hypotheses hold. No truncation, prior or basis enters.

Two facts make this question tractable. First, $\hat\Sigma$ and the moments are both \emph{linear} in the measure, so finding the extremes is a linear program (LP).
Second, every LP has a dual problem. Here the dual variables assemble into a polynomial $p(\lambda)$ that lies above the kernel. This polynomial can be checked after the fact:
if $p\ge g$ on the support, the upper bound holds, regardless of how the solver arrived at it.

\subsection{The optimisation problem}\label{sec:problem}
For a channel $c$ with angular momentum $J$, define $\kappa_J(\lambda)=\frac{2}{E}K_J\big((a^{-1}E)^2/m_\tau^2\big)$ with $E=-\ln\lambda$. Combining
Eq.~(\ref{eq:channels}) with $\dd\sigma=\lambda\hat\rho\,\dd\hat E$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \hat\Sigma_c=\int\dd\sigma(\lambda)\,g(\lambda),\qquad g(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\kappa_J(\lambda)/\lambda,&0<\lambda\le\ee^{-M_0},\\ 0,&\lambda\le0.\end{cases}
 \label{eq:g}
\end{equation}
The function $g$ (black curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:cert}) has three regions, and it helps to keep them apart.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{The kernel window,} $\lambda_c<\lambda\le\ee^{-M_0}$, i.e.\ $M_0\le E<m_\tau$, with $\lambda_c=\ee^{-am_\tau}$ ($0.358$ on 48I, $0.471$ on 64I). Only here is $g>0$.
      It rises steeply towards the kaon; for $J=0$ on 48I, $g(\ee^{-M_0})=7.86$.
\item \emph{Heavy states,} $0\le\lambda\le\lambda_c$, i.e.\ $E\ge m_\tau$. These states are kinematically closed, so $g=0$, but they still contribute to $C(t)$.
\item \emph{Negative eigenvalues,} $-1\le\lambda<0$. These states of the transfer matrix (Sec.~\ref{sec:support}) have no physical energy, and we set $g=0$ for them.
      This is an assumption: we treat them as lattice artefacts that are absent from the continuum spectral function.
\end{itemize}
States in the last two regions \emph{take up part of the moments without contributing to the rate}. This is the mechanism by which the data constrain $\hat\Sigma$ from below as well as from above.
The upper edge $\ee^{-M_0}$, given by the kaon as the lightest $\bar us$ state, is needed because $g\sim2/E$ diverges as $E\to0$. Without it the upper bound would be infinite.

We seek bounds over all positive measures that are consistent with the data:
\begin{equation}
 \hat\Sigma^{\pm}=\ \sup/\inf\ \int g\,\dd\sigma\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sigma\ge0\ \text{on}\ S,\quad |m_k-C(k+1)|\le z\,\delta_{k+1},\ \ k=0,\dots,T-1.
 \label{eq:primal}
\end{equation}
Here $S=[-1,\ee^{-M_0}]$ (new) or $(0,\ee^{-M_0}]$ (old), $m_k=\int\lambda^k\dd\sigma$, and $\delta_t$ is the jackknife error of $C(t)$.
The constraints are $T$ ``boxes'', one per correlator value, each with a half-width of $z$ standard deviations.

\paragraph{Enlarging the support can only widen the interval.} Every measure on $(0,\ee^{-M_0}]$ is also a measure on $[-1,\ee^{-M_0}]$. The new feasible set therefore
contains the old one, and
\begin{equation}
 [\hat\Sigma^-_{\rm old},\hat\Sigma^+_{\rm old}]\subseteq[\hat\Sigma^-_{\rm new},\hat\Sigma^+_{\rm new}]\qquad\text{whenever the old problem is feasible.}
\end{equation}
The new hypothesis is weaker and therefore safer. On our data the cost is small. For $J=0$ the old problem has no solution at all, and for $J=1$ its bounds are only slightly tighter (Table~\ref{tab:bounds}).

\subsection{The dual: where the certificate comes from}\label{sec:dual}
Write $C_k\equiv C(k+1)$ and $\delta_k\equiv\delta_{k+1}$. For the upper bound, attach a multiplier $y^+_k\ge0$ to $m_k\le C_k+z\delta_k$ and
$y^-_k\ge0$ to $-m_k\le-(C_k-z\delta_k)$. For every feasible $\sigma$ and every choice $y^\pm\ge0$, the added terms are non-negative, so
\begin{align}
 \int g\,\dd\sigma\ &\le\ \int g\,\dd\sigma+\sum_k y^+_k\big(C_k+z\delta_k-m_k\big)+\sum_k y^-_k\big(m_k-C_k+z\delta_k\big)\nonumber\\
 &=\int\big(g-p\big)\dd\sigma+\sum_k\big[p_kC_k+z\delta_k(y^+_k+y^-_k)\big],
\end{align}
where $p_k\equiv y^+_k-y^-_k$ and $p(\lambda)=\sum_kp_k\lambda^k$. If $p\ge g$ on $S$, the first term is $\le0$ because $\sigma\ge0$. It is optimal to make at most one of $y^\pm_k$ non-zero,
in which case $y^+_k+y^-_k=|p_k|$. This proves:
\begin{prop}\label{prop:cert}
Let $p(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_k\lambda^k$ satisfy $p\ge g$ on $S$. Then every $\sigma$ that is feasible in (\ref{eq:primal}) satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \int g\,\dd\sigma\ \le\ U(p)\equiv\sum_kp_kC(k+1)+z\sum_k|p_k|\,\delta_{k+1}.
 \label{eq:cert}
\end{equation}
Likewise, if $q\le g$ on $S$, then $\int g\,\dd\sigma\ge L(q)\equiv\sum_kq_kC(k+1)-z\sum_k|q_k|\,\delta_{k+1}$.
\end{prop}
The best certificate minimises $U(p)$ over all polynomials with $p\ge g$ on $S$. This is the \emph{dual} of (\ref{eq:primal}). For the moment problem on a compact interval there is
no duality gap, so $\min_pU(p)=\hat\Sigma^+$. Three properties of the dual help in reading the results.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Sensitivity.} $p_k=\partial\hat\Sigma^+/\partial C(k+1)$, so the certificate tells how much the bound moves when one data point moves. The term $z|p_k|\delta_{k+1}$ is the
      price paid for the error on $C(k+1)$. A polynomial with large alternating coefficients is penalised. This is the noise amplification of Sec.~\ref{sec:cheb} seen from the other side.
\item \emph{Complementary slackness.} At the optimum, the maximising $\sigma$ puts weight only where $p(\lambda)=g(\lambda)$. Also, $y^+_k>0$ only if $m_k$ sits at the upper edge of its box.
      In Fig.~\ref{fig:cert} each polynomial touches $g$ exactly at the atoms of the corresponding extremal measure.
\item \emph{Role of the support.} The condition $p\ge g$ is imposed only on $S$. Under the old support, $p$ is unrestricted for $\lambda<0$. Under the new support, $p\ge0$ is also required on $[-1,0)$.
      More constraints give a larger minimum. This is the dual form of the inclusion above.
\end{itemize}
If $g$ were a polynomial, the condition $p-g\ge0$ on $[-1,1]$ would be equivalent to $p-g=s_0+(1-\lambda^2)s_1$ with sums of squares $s_0,s_1$ (Luk\'acs).
That turns the dual into a semidefinite program, and it is the grid-free version listed under next steps in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{A worked example with two moments}\label{sec:toy}
To see how the bounds arise, take $T=2$ and exact data ($z\delta\to0$). Let $m_0=C(1)$, and let $\mu=C(2)/C(1)$ be the mean of $\lambda$ under the normalised measure $\sigma/m_0$.
The certificate is now a straight line, $p(\lambda)=a+b\lambda$, and $U=m_0(a+b\mu)$ is $m_0$ times its value at $\lambda=\mu$. The lowest line that stays above $g$ on $S$, evaluated at $\mu$,
is the \emph{concave envelope} $\hat g_S(\mu)$. Similarly, the highest line below $g$ gives the convex envelope $\check g_S(\mu)$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 m_0\,\check g_S(\mu)\ \le\ \hat\Sigma\ \le\ m_0\,\hat g_S(\mu).
\end{equation}
For the $A_T$ channel on 48I we have $\mu=0.104$, $\ee^{-M_0}=0.750$ and $g(\ee^{-M_0})=7.86$.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Lower bound.} Since $\mu<\lambda_c=0.358$, a single atom at $\lambda=\mu$, i.e.\ one state above $m_\tau$, reproduces both moments and contributes nothing to the rate. Hence $\hat\Sigma^-=0$:
      two moments cannot tell that a kaon is present.
\item \emph{Upper bound.} $g$ vanishes left of $\lambda_c$ and is convex inside the window, so $\hat g_S$ is the chord from the left end of $S$ to the point $(\ee^{-M_0},g(\ee^{-M_0}))$.
      The maximising measure is an atom at the kaon plus an atom at the left end of $S$. With the old support the left end is $\lambda=0$, and $\hat\Sigma^+=m_0\,g(\ee^{-M_0})\,\mu/\ee^{-M_0}=0.042$\,GeV$^2$.
      With the new support it is $\lambda=-1$, and $\hat\Sigma^+=m_0\,g(\ee^{-M_0})(1+\mu)/(1+\ee^{-M_0})=0.189$\,GeV$^2$.
      An atom at $\lambda=-1$ lowers $m_1$, which leaves room for more weight on the kaon.
\end{itemize}
The LP reproduces both numbers to five digits. Adding moments closes the interval quickly. With the new support and $z=3$:

Once the moments reach that far, the weight at the kaon is pinned, and the interval closes to $\pm2\%$ around the kaon-pole value $0.0208$.
With the old support the same problem has no solution from $T=6$ on.

\subsection{Numerics, and which step is rigorous}\label{sec:numerics}
\paragraph{The linear program.} Because $g$ is not a polynomial, we solve the primal problem on a grid $\{\lambda_i\}$ (function \texttt{make\_grid}). The grid has $3000$ points uniform in $E\in[M_0,3]$,
$1000$ points in $[3,9]$, the points $E=14$ and $40$, and $\lambda=0$. For the new support it also has $900$ points in $[-1,-10^{-4}]$, uniform down to $-0.05$ and geometric below.
The measure is $\sigma=\sum_iw_i\delta_{\lambda_i}$. We use variables $u_i=w_i/C(1)\ge0$ and divide each box by $\delta_{k+1}$, so all rows are of order one:
\begin{equation}
 \max/\min\ \sum_iC(1)g(\lambda_i)\,u_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Big|\sum_i\frac{C(1)\lambda_i^k}{\delta_{k+1}}u_i-\frac{C(k+1)}{\delta_{k+1}}\Big|\le z,\quad k=0,\dots,T-1.
\end{equation}
The LP is solved with HiGHS through \texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog}. Restricting $\sigma$ to a grid can only shrink the feasible set, so the grid bounds are an \emph{inner} approximation:
they can be too narrow, never too wide. The next step repairs this.

\paragraph{The extremal measures have few atoms.} A basic optimal solution has at most as many non-zero $u_i$ as there are active constraints. Since $z>0$, the two sides of a box cannot both be active.
\emph{The extremal measures therefore have at most $T$ atoms.} On the data they have 5--8. This is why Fig.~\ref{fig:cert} shows a few isolated spikes; adjacent grid points occasionally share one atom.

\paragraph{From the grid to a certificate.} The following steps make the bound rigorous (function \texttt{certified\_bounds}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item Read the dual values $y^\pm_k$ of the two rows of box $k$ from the solver and set $p_k=(y^+_k-y^-_k)/\delta_{k+1}$, which undoes the row scaling.
      By LP duality, $p\ge g$ holds at every grid point.
\item Evaluate $p-g$ on a much denser grid of the whole support: $2\times10^5$ points uniform in $\lambda$ over the positive part, the point $\lambda=0$, and $5\times10^4$ points in $[-1,0)$.
\item Set $\Delta=\max(0,\max(g-p))$ and shift $p_0\to p_0+\Delta$. Now $p\ge g$ holds on the dense grid.
\item Evaluate $U(p)$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:cert}). For the lower bound, repeat with $q$, $\Delta=\max(0,\max(q-g))$, $q_0\to q_0-\Delta$, and $L(q)$.
\end{enumerate}
The shift moves the bound by about $\Delta\cdot C(1)$. On the data the certified and grid bounds differ by at most $3\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{GeV}^2$ (Table~\ref{tab:bounds}).
The dense-grid check is numerical, not an analytic proof that $p\ge g$. However, $g$ is smooth in each region and $p$ is a polynomial of degree at most $17$, so a violation would have to fit
between grid points spaced by $3\times10^{-6}$. The sum-of-squares formulation above would remove this caveat.

\paragraph{Statistical meaning.} Each box has half-width $z\delta_t$. With Gaussian errors, all $T$ boxes contain the true values with probability at least
$1-T\cdot0.0027$ for $z=3$ (Bonferroni), i.e.\ at least $95\%$ for $T=18$. The covariance between different $t$ is not used. Including it, as an ellipsoid instead of a box, would shrink the feasible set
and tighten the bounds.

\paragraph{Choice of $T$.} At fixed $z$, each additional moment adds a constraint. The interval can therefore only shrink as $T$ grows, and the bound is valid for every $T$.
Once $\delta_t/C(t)$ becomes large, the new box is too wide to ever be active, and the bounds stop improving (Fig.~\ref{fig:bounds}). We use $T=18$. The only cost of a larger $T$ is the Bonferroni factor.

\paragraph{Calibration.} For a single state ($m=0.4937$\,GeV, $f=0.1564$\,GeV on 48I, relative errors $10^{-3}$) the method gives $\Sigma\in[0.02075,0.02089]\,\mathrm{GeV}^2$
for both supports, around the exact value $f^2(1-M^2/m_\tau^2)^2=0.02083$. The certificate reproduces the grid bound to five digits.

\subsection{Feasibility}\label{sec:feas}
Before computing bounds we ask whether any positive measure on $S$ reproduces the data at all. The smallest tolerance $z^*$ for which this is possible is again an LP:
minimise $\max_t|\sum_i w_i\lambda_i^{t-1}-C(t)|/\delta_t$ over $w\ge0$.

\subsection{The recipe}\label{sec:recipe}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item For each channel, build $C_{\rm ren}(t)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:norm}) and its jackknife error $\delta_t$ for $t=1,\dots,T$.
\item Build the grid on $S=[-1,\ee^{-M_0}]$ and evaluate $g(\lambda)$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:g}) for the channel's $J$.
\item Compute $z^*$. If $z^*>z$, the hypothesis is rejected for this channel and no bound is computed.
\item Solve the two LPs (\ref{eq:primal}) and read off $p$ and $q$ from the dual values.
\item Check $p\ge g\ge q$ on the dense grid, shift by $\Delta$, and compute $U(p)$ and $L(q)$. These are $\hat\Sigma^\pm_c$.
\item Add the four channels: $\Sigma^\pm_{\rm tot}=(a^{-1})^2\sum_c\hat\Sigma^\pm_c$. This is conservative, because each channel may sit at its own extreme.
\item Convert to $\Vus\in\big[\sqrt{c/\Sigma^+_{\rm tot}},\sqrt{c/\Sigma^-_{\rm tot}}\big]$ with $c=\BR_s\Gamma_\tau/(S_{EW}G_F^2m_\tau^3/16\pi)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:vus}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Testing the hypothesis: Hausdorff positivity}\label{sec:hausdorff}
The feasibility test of Sec.~\ref{sec:feas} uses all moments together. This section gives an independent and more local test, based on the classical characterisation of moment sequences.

\subsection{Necessary conditions}
Let $P(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^nx_i\lambda^i$ be a real polynomial and $s_k=\int\dd\sigma\,\lambda^k\,\lambda^{t_s-1}$. If $\sigma\ge0$ on $[-1,1]$ and $t_s$ is odd, then
\begin{align}
 x^{\!\top}H x&=\sum_{ij}x_ix_j\,s_{i+j}=\int\dd\sigma\,\lambda^{t_s-1}P(\lambda)^2\ \ge0,
 &H_{ij}&=s_{i+j},\label{eq:H0}\\
 x^{\!\top}H^{\rm loc}x&=\int\dd\sigma\,\lambda^{t_s-1}(1-\lambda^2)P(\lambda)^2\ \ge0,
 &H^{\rm loc}_{ij}&=s_{i+j}-s_{i+j+2}.\label{eq:Hloc}
\end{align}
The Hankel matrix $H$ and the localizing matrix $H^{\rm loc}$ must therefore be positive semidefinite. For support $[0,1]$ the localizing matrix is instead $H^{\rm loc}_{ij}=s_{i+j+1}-s_{i+j+2}$, from the weight $\lambda(1-\lambda)$.
Schm\"udgen \cite{Schmudgen:2020} shows that for $s_0,\dots,s_{2n}$ these conditions are also \emph{sufficient}, with no rank condition on a compact interval.
Here only necessity matters. A significantly negative eigenvalue \emph{excludes} the hypothesis, while passing the test means compatibility, not proof.

\subsection{Scaling and statistics}
The values $s_k$ decay by orders of magnitude, so we scale $H\to D^{-1}HD^{-1}$ with $D=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{|H_{ii}|})$. This is a congruence, so by Sylvester's law it does not change positive semidefiniteness.
For each $(t_s,n)$ we compute the smallest eigenvalue $\mu_{\min}$ and its jackknife error over configurations. We call a matrix a \emph{violation} if $\mu_{\min}/\sigma<-3$ and a \emph{pass} if $\mu_{\min}/\sigma>3$.
The rows of the data files carry no configuration label. The autocorrelation along the row order is at the noise level
($|{\rm ac}|<0.05$ for lags $1$--$100$), so the jackknife error does not depend on the assumed ordering.

\section{Why the old Chebyshev coefficients leave $[-1,1]$}\label{sec:cheb}
\subsection{Definitions and the old relation}
With $\lambda=(1+\cos\theta)/2=\cos^2(\theta/2)$, the shifted Chebyshev polynomials are $T^*_j(\lambda)=\cos j\theta=T_j(2\lambda-1)$.
Normalise $\bar C(t)=C(t+2t_0)/C(2t_0)=m_t/m_0\equiv\langle\lambda^t\rangle$. Expanding $\lambda^t$ in Fourier modes,
\begin{align}
 \lambda^t&=\cos^{2t}\!\tfrac\theta2=2^{-2t}\!\!\sum_{k=0}^{2t}\binom{2t}{k}\ee^{\ii(t-k)\theta}
 =2^{1-2t}\Big[\tfrac12\binom{2t}{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\binom{2t}{t-j}\cos j\theta\Big],\\
 \bar C(t)&=2^{1-2t}\Big[\tfrac12\binom{2t}{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\binom{2t}{t-j}\langle T^*_j\rangle\Big].\label{eq:cbar}
\end{align}
This is Eq.~(56) of Ref.~\cite{Chen:TauNote}, with $\langle T^*_0\rangle=1$ by normalisation. The old method fits the $\langle T^*_j\rangle$ to $\bar C(t)$, $t=1,\dots,T$,
with the prior $|\langle T^*_j\rangle|<1$ imposed through an $\mathrm{erfinv}$ map.

\subsection{The bound, and how negative eigenvalues break it}
\begin{lemma}
If $\sigma\ge0$ is normalised on $[0,1]$, then $|\langle T^*_n\rangle|\le1$. If $\sigma\ge0$ is normalised on $[-1,1]$, then $|\langle T_n\rangle|\le1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$|T^*_n|\le1$ on $[0,1]$ and $|T_n|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$, so $|\langle T\rangle|\le\int|T|\,\dd\sigma\le\int\dd\sigma=1$.
\end{proof}
Now place a small weight $w$ at $\lambda=-\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon>0$. Since $2\lambda-1=-1-2\varepsilon$ lies outside $[-1,1]$, the Chebyshev polynomial grows exponentially there:
\begin{equation}
 T^*_n(-\varepsilon)=(-1)^n\cosh\!\big(n\,\mathrm{arccosh}(1+2\varepsilon)\big)\simeq\tfrac12(-1)^n\ee^{\,n\kappa},\quad \kappa=\mathrm{arccosh}(1+2\varepsilon).
\end{equation}
The contribution $w\,T^*_n(-\varepsilon)$ alone exceeds $1$ once $n\gtrsim\ln(2/w)/\kappa$. For $\varepsilon=0.1$ ($\kappa=0.62$) this happens at
$n\gtrsim8.5$ for $w=1\%$ and at $n\gtrsim4.8$ for $w=10\%$. A small negative-$\lambda$ component is therefore enough to push the old
coefficients out of $[-1,1]$ at the orders observed in the data. The extremal measures of Sec.~\ref{sec:extremal} have exactly such components, at $\lambda\approx-0.1$ with $7$--$19\%$ of the weight.

\subsection{Noise amplification}
The monomial coefficients $c_k$ of $T^*_n(x)$ alternate in sign, because all roots lie in $(0,1)$ (Descartes' rule). Hence $\sum_k|c_k|=|T^*_n(-1)|=T_n(3)\simeq\tfrac12(3+2\sqrt2)^n$.
The coefficients of $T_n(x)$ alternate in $x^2$, so $\sum_k|c_k|=|T_n(\ii)|\simeq\tfrac12(1+\sqrt2)^n$. At $n=12$ these sums are $7.7\times10^{8}$ and
$2.0\times10^{4}$. The $[-1,1]$ basis therefore amplifies the statistical noise of the moments about $4\times10^4$ times less.
The price is a coarser resolution in $\lambda$, roughly a factor of two in polynomial degree.

\section{What is and is not shown}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item The $(0,1]$ support is \emph{excluded} by the $J=0$ data at $\ge3.3\sigma$ (48I) and $\ge9\sigma$ (64I), while $[-1,1]$ is compatible. This conclusion does not depend on any value of $\Vus$.
\item The $\Vus$ interval is wide ($\approx\pm11\%$). It is a guarantee, not a competitive determination, and the $J=1$ axial channel limits it.
\item The interval is valid only if (i) $\sigma\ge0$ on $[-1,1]$ holds for this action (tested at odd $t_s$, not proven), (ii) the lowest state is the kaon, (iii) $\BR_s$ (HFLAV 2023), $Z$, $a^{-1}$ and $M_0$ are correct,
      and (iv) the Gaussian error model holds. With $z=3$ and $T=18$ the statistical confidence is at least $95\%$.
\item Not included: the continuum extrapolation (each ensemble has a single lattice spacing), the errors of $a^{-1}$ ($\approx0.2\%$ on $\Vus$) and of $Z$, the full covariance (boxes are used instead),
      lattice artefacts of the local currents, and finite-volume effects beyond the positivity of the measure.
\item Next steps: an exact free-field M\"obius check at $M_5=1.8$, where a single negative pivot would be decisive; a sum-of-squares (SDP) implementation with an exact, grid-free certificate;
      and the full covariance as an ellipsoid constraint, which would tighten the bounds.
\end{itemize}
